\begin{center}{\large\bfseries Abstract}\end{center}
\begin{quote}\small
A neural correction of a satellite estimate must decide both what to predict and when its correction is useful.
We study this decision as selective residual regression under spatial transfer and input corruption.
Using 7,923 OpenET station-days from 85 stations, we compare ensemble disagreement, input-support distance, and learned relative benefit.
Selectors use spatially cross-fitted neural predictions; a separate evaluation withholds both locations and later years.
The experiment retains a fixed satellite fallback and scores every test observation.
A natural humidity anomaly motivates a separate feature-omission arm and controlled test-input faults.
After VPD omission, a tenfold wind-input fault raises residual-network station MAE from 0.829 to 1.415 $\unit$ on 7,873 matched observations.
Support screening yields 0.857, but largely through fallback: it accepts only 1.74\% of the station-weighted population.
At matched 50\% acceptance, its advantage over disagreement is inconclusive.
However, the learned benefit ranking is worse than the support ranking by 0.243 $\unit$, with paired interval [0.110, 0.412], under that same fault.
The later-year evaluation does not confirm this ordering, and natural-test gains do not transfer uniformly to croplands.
We derive the baseline-relative risk objective, preserve all negative controls, and distinguish screening from ranking improvements.
The result isolates a failure of cross-fitted benefit selection under a specified input fault and separates correction ranking from fallback frequency.
\end{quote}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} selective regression; neural residual learning; model deferral; input corruption; evapotranspiration

\section{Introduction}
Machine Learning Evapotranspiration (MLET) combines satellite estimates, meteorological covariates, and auditable environmental prediction.
A satellite model already provides an actual-ET estimate where direct flux measurements are unavailable.
A neural residual model can correct its error, but that correction can also damage a useful estimate.
The technical question is therefore conditional: \emph{when should the system apply the learned correction at an unseen location?}

This question connects environmental modeling to regression with deferral.
An uncertainty score estimates reliability of a model, whereas the correction decision compares that model with an available fallback.
Low neural error does not necessarily mean improvement over the satellite estimate.
Moreover, a learned selector faces its own distribution shift when local weather inputs or their support change.

We investigate this narrow problem using matched neural predictions and a fixed OpenET fallback.
We compare a disagreement threshold, a training-support threshold, and a learned absolute-loss benefit selector.
A crossed experiment separates geographic withholding, later years, and omission of a previously identified anomalous covariate.
Paired input faults then isolate selection behavior while holding labels and neural parameters fixed.
The contribution is a reproducible empirical study of selector transfer, supported by an explicit loss objective and matched-acceptance diagnostics.

Agricultural use requires a separate population check: success across mixed land covers need not imply success on croplands.
We therefore report cropland transfer alongside the full archive and retain the satellite estimate as a deployable fallback concept.
The evaluation is retrospective; it does not test operational availability or irrigation interventions.

The study makes three connected empirical contributions.
First, it evaluates selection by improvement over the fallback, with spatially cross-fitted targets and complete-system loss.
Second, paired input faults separate neural error, rejection frequency, and correction ranking while holding the fitted predictor fixed.
Third, matched acceptance exposes an inconclusive uncertainty comparison and a specific benefit-selector failure that later-year data do not confirm.
Appendices~\ref{app:architecture} and~\ref{app:outlook} retain the original MLET architecture and reference-ETo visuals as historical context.

\section{Related work}
\citet{mao2024} formulate regression with multi-expert deferral, including two-stage methods and consistency analysis.
This is the closest formal setting: MLET chooses between a neural correction and a satellite predictor.
We use a direct, cross-fitted loss-difference regressor as a fixed baseline.
We do not reproduce their surrogate losses or claim their consistency bounds for this unbounded absolute-loss experiment.

\citet{sokol2024} study conformalized selective regression and connect uncertainty with coverage.
Our objective includes the fallback's error on rejected observations, rather than report accuracy only on retained predictions.
We claim no conformal coverage guarantee under geographic shift.
We use seed disagreement following \citet{laksh2016}.
Its behavior can be unreliable outside training support \citep{mathelin2023}.

Applicability-domain methods relate environmental prediction reliability to covariate support \citep{meyer2020}.
GeoQ uses geometry, cross-fitting, and conditional quantile increments to estimate scientific-surrogate errors \citep{nguyen2026}.
Our distance control is simpler; it tests whether support screening changes the interpretation of a learned selector.
Work on ReLU extrapolation further motivates separating interpolation from unsupported input directions \citep{xu2020}.
Graph neural processes demonstrate environmental prediction at sparse sensor locations \citep{hu2023}.
Their graph and temporal context differs from the sparse satellite-date regression studied here.


\citet{volk2024} evaluate OpenET against a public collection of measured ET.
The associated model and measurement archives provide the observations used here \citep{openetdata,fluxdata}.
Our reuse of that archive does not constitute an independent evaluation of upstream OpenET model development.
The held-out partitions apply to the additional correction models trained in this study.

Machine learning correction of ET is already an established research direction.
\citet{hascoet2022} learn corrections using water-cycle closure supervision.
\citet{rozanov2025} combine physical features, tree models, and neural models to estimate ET across the U.S. Midwest.
\citet{shi2024} study domain-adversarial learning for geographical ET transfer.
Recent work also studies residual correction of physical ET estimates and physically constrained learning \citep{residual2026,wei2026}.
These studies motivate strong correction baselines; they do not establish that a particular correction transfers in the present archive.
OpenET also documents land-cover-specific biases, including forest overprediction \citep{openetissues}.

Structured validation must reflect the dependence present in environmental data \citep{roberts2017}.
Nearby towers can share weather, management, and landscape conditions despite having different station identifiers.
We therefore complement station withholding with proximity groups.
We also distinguish site-year grouping from simultaneously excluding a station and all later test years.
The latter prevents training on earlier observations of a test station or on later observations from other stations.

Reference ET is a separate quantity defined for a standardized surface.
\citet{kukal2026} question replacing its specified calculation with a learned approximation.
Our target is measured actual ET; reference ET is only an input.
We do not infer crop water need, irrigation response, or reference-ET forecast skill from these results.

JEPA-Anything studies factorized predictive representations across domains \citep{cui2026}.
Dreamer studies learned dynamics for control \citep{hafner2025}.
They motivate explicit predictive structure and tests that isolate a proposed mechanism.
They do not justify labeling a same-date scalar regression as an action-conditioned world model.
The current archive lacks a matched irrigation-action sequence and a corresponding evaluation of intervention outcomes.
Our decomposition concerns correction error, not identified causal factors.

\section{Data and evaluation}
\subsection{A fixed retrospective cohort}
We use the OpenET model archive, Zenodo record 10119477, and the processed flux archive, record 7636781.
Both downloaded archives match the checksums in the existing MLET manifest.
The exact station-date join produces 16,447 rows from 152 stations.
Of these, 16,444 contain a measured, non-gap-filled corrected ET label.
Requiring finite reference ET, temperature, vapor-pressure deficit (VPD), and wind leaves 7,923 observations from 85 stations.
This reproduces the earlier complete-weather cohort exactly.

The selected rows span 415 station-years from 2001 through 2020.
They are satellite validation dates, not complete daily sequences.
The target is the archive's \texttt{ET\_corr}, which incorporates its energy-balance correction procedure.
We retain negative target values and do not clip predictions.
The complete-weather subset contains 32 cropland stations and 2,670 observations.
The other stations represent grasslands, shrublands, forests, and wetland or riparian settings.
Cropland classification does not establish irrigation status.

Let $y_{st}$ denote corrected measured ET and $o_{st}$ the OpenET ensemble at station $s$ and date $t$.
The input vector is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x_{st}&=[o_{st},ETo_{st},h_t,T_{st},VPD_{st},u_{st}],\\
h_t&=[\sin(2\pi d_t/365),\cos(2\pi d_t/365)].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $d_t$ is day of year, $T$ is average air temperature, and $u$ is wind speed.
The reference-ET covariate is gridMET ETo from the original joined table.
We retain the earlier 365-day season encoding for reproducibility.
The meteorological measurements and satellite estimates refer to the target date.
Their operational release times are not reconstructed.
Accordingly, this is retrospective estimation, not forecasting.

\subsection{Three outer evaluations}
All split assignments use seed 20260713.
The first evaluation reproduces the original station-held-out 10-fold evaluation.
No station contributes rows to both training and testing in one fold.

The primary evaluation constructs an undirected graph over the 85 stations.
An edge connects stations whose great-circle distance is at most 10 km.
Connected components define 63 proximity groups, including transitive connections.
We allocate these groups to ten folds, keeping each component intact.
All 7,923 observations receive exactly one held-out prediction per model.
The 10 km threshold is fixed before this model comparison.
It is a local-dependence check, not a test of transfer to an unseen climate region.

The joint evaluation uses the same proximity folds.
Training additionally excludes dates from 2019 onward.
Testing includes only dates from 2019 onward in held-out groups.
This yields 649 observations from 27 stations and 24 groups.
Only nine test stations are croplands, contributing 403 observations in seven groups.
Comparisons within this evaluation use identical rows.
Comparisons with the primary evaluation also reflect a changed test cohort.

\subsection{Nested selection and inference}
Inner selection uses three disjoint proximity-group folds drawn solely from the outer training set.
In the joint evaluation, inner training ends before 2016 and validation starts in 2016.
Outer test observations never determine scaling, stopping, regularization, or correction strength.
We save the row identifiers for every inner and outer partition.

Our primary mean absolute error (MAE) gives equal weight to each test station:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MAE}_{\mathrm{station}}(f)=
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\frac{1}{n_s}\sum_{t=1}^{n_s}|y_{st}-f(x_{st})|.
\label{eq:macro}
\end{equation}
We also report pooled MAE, pooled root mean square error (RMSE), and signed bias.
Pooled MAE instead weights station $s$ by $n_s/N$.
These scores answer different questions when sampling density varies.
Neither estimates an area-weighted agricultural error.

We report paired differences as baseline error minus candidate error.
Positive differences favor the candidate.
Intervals use 2,000 bootstrap samples of proximity components, with seed 20260922.
Each sampled component contributes all its stations; repeated components retain their multiplicity.
This preserves local clusters and avoids treating thousands of dates as independent replicates.
The percentile intervals condition on the fitted cross-validation models.
They omit retraining uncertainty and dependence between different proximity components.
The exploratory comparisons have no multiplicity adjustment.

\section{Neural correction models}\label{sec:models}
\subsection{Matched comparisons}
We include the training median and the earlier crop-coefficient baseline in the accompanying results.
Weather ridge uses reference ET, season, temperature, VPD, and wind.
Combined ridge adds OpenET.
Both standardize inputs using training means and standard deviations, then use ridge penalty $\alpha=1$.
Tuned ridge chooses $\alpha\in\{1,10,100,1000\}$ by inner station-macro MAE.
Affine calibration fits an intercept and slope to OpenET alone.
Unchanged OpenET learns no parameters from this cohort.

Histogram gradient boosting supplies a nonlinear weather-only baseline.
We also fit matched direct and residual boosting models with the complete input vector.
Both use 150 iterations, learning rate 0.05, seven leaves, a 30-row minimum leaf size, and L2 regularization 10.
Internal random-row early stopping is disabled.

The neural comparisons use two 32-unit ReLU layers and a scalar output.
They have 1,345 trainable parameters with seven inputs.
Direct and residual networks use identical architectures, optimizers, epoch counts, and seeds.
We use Adam, learning rate 0.001, batch size 256, and 120 epochs.
The scikit-learn L2 penalty parameter is 0.001.
Inputs and training targets are standardized separately within each fit.
No test-based stopping or architecture search occurs.
We retain results for seeds 20260713, 20260714, and 20260715.
The main neural predictions average the three seeds.
Single-seed scores appear in Appendix~\ref{app:seeds}.

\subsection{Residual prediction is a testable prior}
A direct model predicts $y$.
A residual model predicts $r=y-o$ and returns $o+g(x)$.
The unchanged satellite estimate supplies an explicit fallback:
\begin{equation}
\widehat y_\lambda=o+\lambda g(x),\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq1.
\label{eq:correction}
\end{equation}
This construction does not make the residual predictable or transferable.
For expressive model classes, direct and residual formulations can represent the same functions.
Their practical differences arise from optimization, regularization, and inductive bias.
They must therefore be compared with matched capacity and information.

\begin{proposition}[Correction risk]
For a fixed correction $g$ and any target distribution with finite second moments,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R(\lambda)-\mathcal R(0)=\lambda^2 B-2\lambda A,
\qquad A=\E[rg],\quad B=\E[g^2],
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal R(\lambda)=\E[(y-o-\lambda g)^2]$.
If $B>0$, the minimizing weight on $[0,1]$ is $\lambda^*=\operatorname{clip}(A/B,0,1)$.
If $B=0$, the correction has no effect almost surely.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand $(r-\lambda g)^2-r^2$ and take expectations.
The resulting convex quadratic has unconstrained minimizer $A/B$ when $B>0$.
Projection onto $[0,1]$ gives the constrained minimizer.
\end{proof}

The identity distinguishes useful residual alignment, $A$, from correction energy, $B$.
A full update helps squared error only if $2A>B$.
A large correction without corresponding residual alignment damages the estimate.
These moments also depend on the target population.
Alignment measured across mixed land covers need not persist within croplands or later years.

The gated boosting model estimates $A$ and $B$ from inner out-of-group predictions, using equal station weights.
It fixes $\widehat\lambda$ before fitting the residual model on all outer training data.
This is a familiar scalar shrinkage calculation.
It has no distribution-free guarantee under station or temporal shift.
It also optimizes squared loss, while the primary reporting metric is MAE.
That mismatch is deliberate and must remain visible in the interpretation.

\subsection{Why architecture and input support interact}
Training standardization does not bound an unseen input.
For linear prediction, a test perturbation $\delta$ changes the estimate by $\beta^\top D^{-1}\delta$, where $D$ contains training scales.
An extreme coordinate can therefore dominate the prediction even when all coefficients use ridge regularization.
ReLU networks are piecewise affine and can also extrapolate along unsupported directions.
A residual connection preserves the base estimate only when its learned update remains controlled.

A finite tree ensemble behaves differently for an input perturbation.
Each tree returns a value from its finite set of leaves.
For fixed trees $h_j$ and positive learning rate $\eta$, its output satisfies
\begin{equation}
b+\eta\sum_j\min_\ell h_{j\ell}
\ \leq\ b+\eta\sum_j h_j(x)
\ \leq\ b+\eta\sum_j\max_\ell h_{j\ell}.
\end{equation}
An extreme weather value therefore cannot create unbounded tree output for that fixed fit.
This bound does not guarantee accurate predictions or prevent harmful training influence.
It explains why input validity can interact with model family without establishing that one family is generally superior.

\section{Selective neural residual correction}\label{sec:selective}
\subsection{Predicting benefit differs from predicting error}
Let $o(x)$ denote the available satellite estimate and $g(x)$ a fitted neural residual.
A selector $a(x)\in\{0,1\}$ returns
\begin{equation}
 f_a(x)=o(x)+a(x)g(x).
\end{equation}
Every query receives a prediction, including rejected corrections.
Define the realized absolute-loss improvement as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
 D(x,y)=|y-o(x)|-|y-o(x)-g(x)|.
\end{equation}
Positive $D$ means that the correction helps.
For a fixed fitted model, the complete-system risk satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:selective-risk}
 R(a)=R(o)-\E[a(X)D(X,Y)].
\end{equation}
Thus, the conditional optimal decision applies the correction when $\mu(x)=\E[D(X,Y)\mid X=x]>0$.
A selector that predicts small neural error need not predict positive improvement over $o$.
This distinction is central to regression with deferral \citep{mao2024}; we do not claim it as a new decision rule.

Consider one query with $y=o=0$ and $g=0.1$.
A second query has $(y,o,g)=(10,0,9)$.
The neural absolute errors are 0.1 and 1, but their improvements are $-0.1$ and 9.
Rejecting the larger neural error retains the harmful correction and discards the helpful correction.
Ensemble spread is further removed from this decision: it measures disagreement between fitted networks, not either realized error.

The selector's target can be stated precisely.
For $a^*(x)=\mathbf{1}\{\mu(x)>0\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:selector-regret}
 R(a)-R(a^*)=\E[|\mu(X)|\mathbf{1}\{a(X)\ne a^*(X)\}].
\end{equation}
Conditioning Equation~\ref{eq:selective-risk} on $X$ proves the identity.
Selection errors matter in proportion to the lost improvement, not their count alone.
The triangle inequality also gives $|D|\le |g|$.
Consequently, an accepted correction can worsen absolute loss by no more than its magnitude.
This bound does not establish non-inferiority, and a support threshold does not change that limitation.
These standard loss identities specify the experiment's estimand rather than provide new consistency theory.

\subsection{Spatial cross-fitting of the selector}
The experiment fixes the three-seed residual ensemble from Section~\ref{sec:models}.
Each outer training set produces three inner, geographically separated neural prediction sets.
The joint evaluation also preserves its inner time cutoff.
Only these inner predictions supply selector targets and thresholds.
After selection, the neural ensemble is refitted on the complete outer training set.
No outer target enters a fit, threshold, input scaler, or stopping decision.
Figure~\ref{fig:selector-design} shows this separation.

For residual predictions $g_1,g_2,g_3$, we use their mean $\bar g$ and sample spread
\begin{equation}
 s_g(x)=\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{3}(g_j(x)-\bar g(x))^2\right]^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
This is a small mean-only ensemble inspired by \citet{laksh2016}; it is not their heteroscedastic likelihood model.
A second score measures input support:
\begin{equation}
 d_5(x)=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{z\in N_5(x)}\|S(x)-S(z)\|_2,
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the training-only standardizer and $N_5$ contains five training neighbors.
The neighbor index is rebuilt inside every inner and outer training partition.
Threshold calibration uses held-out inner distances, never distances from training examples to themselves.
This simple control follows applicability-domain reasoning \citep{meyer2020}; it does not reproduce weighted AOA or GeoQ.

The \emph{Spread95} and \emph{Support95} selectors accept scores below their station-weighted inner 95th percentiles.
The \emph{Gain} selector predicts Equation~\ref{eq:gain} with a fixed boosted-tree regressor.
Its six inputs are $(\bar g,|\bar g|,s_g,\log(1+d_5),o,\mathrm{ETo})$.
It uses 80 iterations, learning rate 0.05, seven leaves, minimum leaf size 50, L2 parameter 10, and no early stopping.
Training weights give equal total weight to each station and are normalized to mean one.
Gain accepts a correction only when its predicted improvement is positive.
\emph{SupportGain} requires both Gain and Support95 acceptance.
The neural estimator remains identical across these selectors.

We include two controls.
\emph{Uniform} chooses one multiplier from $\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ using inner station MAE.
\emph{Clip} uses the unmodified fitted neural model but clips weather inputs to outer-training first and 99th percentiles at inference.
Unchanged OpenET and the full residual ensemble define the endpoints.
No selector hyperparameters are adjusted after viewing the outer results.

\subsection{Natural transfer and controlled input faults}
The selective experiment crosses two input specifications with two evaluations: proximity withholding and joint proximity-and-time withholding.
The specifications contain seven archived inputs or six inputs without VPD.
Each natural comparison retains every outer target.
We score the complete system, including fallback rows, and report croplands separately.
The 95\% inner thresholds do not impose 95\% outer acceptance under distribution shift.

Three additional probes change test inputs separately: VPD multiplied by 10, wind multiplied by 10, and temperature increased by $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
Their labels, satellite estimates, neural parameters, and selector parameters remain fixed.
Only probe rows with nonnegative exported actual vapor pressure are eligible.
Each fault is compared with its identical unmodified probe rows.
The VPD probe is an exact negative control when VPD is omitted.
These are synthetic input faults, not interventions on weather and not estimates of fault prevalence.
The protocol specifies their magnitudes before the new run.

\section{Selective transfer results}\label{sec:selection-results}
\subsection{Screening and learning benefit behave differently}
Table~\ref{tab:selective-natural} reports every fixed selector on the natural test rows.
Under proximity withholding with archived inputs, the full residual ensemble scores 1.099 station MAE.
Spread95 and Support95 score 0.817 and 0.818 $\unit$, respectively.
They screen the known large neural failure, but neither improvement interval excludes zero against unchanged OpenET at 0.856.
The learned Gain selector instead scores 1.126.
Predicting relative benefit does not itself make that predictor reliable outside its calibration support.

After VPD omission, the full ensemble scores 0.835, Spread95 scores 0.832, and Support95 scores 0.838.
Their improvement intervals remain inconclusive against OpenET.
The simple Uniform control scores 0.816, with an exploratory improvement interval of [0.002, 0.093].
Its cropland error remains higher than the baseline.
Thus, complexity in the selector is not necessary for the best observed point score in this comparison.
Figure~\ref{fig:selective-transfer} retains all four evaluation settings.

The joint evaluation changes the interpretation again.
For archived inputs, Uniform selects zero in every outer fold and exactly returns OpenET.
The full ensemble nevertheless has lower observed station MAE on the later outer years, 0.766 versus 0.870.
An earlier validation interval can reject corrections that later help on average.
After VPD omission, the joint Gain, SupportGain, and full-model intervals all include zero.
These results give no general guarantee for the learned selector or the fallback rule.

\subsection{Input support controls a specific neural fault}
The unmodified probe subset contains 7,873 observations at 84 stations in 62 proximity groups.
After VPD omission, its full-ensemble station MAE is 0.829.
Multiplying wind inputs by ten raises that error to 1.415 $\unit$.
Spread95 scores 0.914 and Support95 scores 0.857, against 0.853 for unchanged OpenET.
These are errors on identical probe rows, with identical fitted models and unchanged targets.
The fault changes only the supplied wind coordinate.

The two thresholds spend different correction budgets.
Spread95 accepts 19.96\% of the station-weighted wind-fault population, whereas Support95 accepts 1.74\%.
On the natural no-VPD population, their acceptance rates are 95.74\% and 96.84\%.
The small Support95 fault error therefore depends substantially on returning to the baseline.
It is not evidence that its accepted neural predictions have uniformly superior accuracy.

The learned Gain selector accepts 56.95\% under the wind fault and scores 1.261 station MAE.
Adding the support condition reduces acceptance to 1.40\% and yields 0.856.
A selector trained on relative loss can itself extrapolate into a poorly represented score configuration.
A separate support condition changes that behavior without retraining the neural predictor.

The temperature fault supplies a negative control on generality.
Support95 scores 0.904 under the $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ offset, still worse than OpenET at 0.853.
Figure~\ref{fig:input-faults} and Table~\ref{tab:selective-faults} report this failure alongside the wind result.
VPD corruption changes no predictions in the no-VPD arm, as required by the implementation's exact negative control.

\subsection{Matched budgets limit the stronger interpretation}
The fixed-threshold results motivate a post hoc ranking diagnostic with equal station-weighted acceptance budgets.
Spread and support scores are divided by their respective inner 95th-percentile thresholds before pooling folds.
Predicted gain is ranked from largest to smallest.
The outer score distribution supplies the ranks without labels.
A randomized boundary observation gives an exact acceptance budget.
We score its expected loss, rather than interpolate its two predictions.
This is a transductive ranking diagnostic; it is not the deployment rule evaluated above.

At 50\% acceptance on the no-VPD wind probe, Support ranks yield 1.000 expected station MAE, versus 1.023 for Spread.
Their difference is 0.023 $\unit$, with paired interval $[-0.022,0.066]$.
Thus, the apparent fixed-threshold support advantage does not establish a superior uncertainty ranking at equal coverage.
This negative result is essential to the interpretation.

The Gain ranking fails more clearly in this specific probe.
At the same 50\% budget it scores 1.243, versus 1.000 for Support.
The paired difference is 0.243 $\unit$, with interval [0.110, 0.412].
On the identical unmodified rows, Gain scores 0.835 versus 0.867 for Support.
That point advantage is 0.031, with interval $[-0.0003,0.0690]$; it is not decisive evidence of natural-data superiority.
The reversal identifies a failure of the fitted benefit selector under a specified covariate fault, after removing the known VPD anomaly.

The joint wind comparison does not confirm the same advantage: Gain minus Support is 0.032, with interval $[-0.105,0.194]$.
Temperature-fault comparisons are also inconclusive.
Figure~\ref{fig:matched-acceptance} gives the complete curves, and the saved records include every input specification and fault.
No curve or acceptance budget is used to refit a model.

\subsection{Cropland transfer is a separate ML target}
The natural cropland subset contains 2,670 observations from 32 stations in 18 proximity groups.
After VPD omission, OpenET scores 0.931 station MAE, while the full residual ensemble scores 0.997 $\unit$.
All selective alternatives have higher cropland point error than OpenET; uniform shrinkage is closest at 0.961 $\unit$.
Its paired improvement interval is $[-0.074,0.017]$, so this comparison does not establish a cropland benefit.
The later-year cropland subset contains 403 observations from nine stations in seven groups.
There, no-VPD SupportGain scores 0.790 against 0.785 for OpenET, with improvement interval $[-0.035,0.017]$.
Appendix~\ref{app:selective-tables} reports every method and both input specifications.
These results limit the agricultural interpretation: identifying a correction failure is useful evidence, but it does not validate a better cropland estimator.

\section{Discussion and limits}
\subsection{What the narrow result establishes}
The fitted benefit selector can transfer differently from the neural predictor it controls.
In the no-VPD wind probe, a correct relative-loss training objective does not prevent the selector from accepting damaging corrections.
Support screening suppresses that specific failure, but much of its fixed-threshold advantage comes from greater fallback.
Matching acceptance separates two questions: whether the selector rejects unfamiliar inputs and whether it ranks useful corrections better.
The support-versus-disagreement ranking difference is inconclusive at the specified 50\% budget.
The larger Gain-versus-Support difference applies to one spatial probe and does not confirm in the smaller later-year cohort.

For agricultural deployment, selection must improve the error of the relevant crop population.
The cropland results show why an aggregate mixed-land-cover score cannot supply that evidence.
The present result supports diagnostics for correction behavior; it does not establish an agricultural deployment gain.

\subsection{Scope of inference}
All new analyses extend an inspected benchmark.
The selective protocol is fixed before its run, but follows knowledge of the covariate anomaly and prior outcomes.
The matched-budget analysis is specified after the fixed-threshold results and is explicitly post hoc.
The bootstrap conditions on fitted models and selector ranks, omits retraining uncertainty, and has no multiplicity correction.
The 62 probe groups, rather than 7,873 days, supply its main independent resampling units.

The three-seed ensemble measures initialization variation, not calibrated posterior uncertainty.
The neural architecture and selectors have fixed budgets rather than exhaustive tuning.
No matched implementation of a leading deferral surrogate, neural process, or GeoQ model is evaluated.
The result cannot establish superiority over those methods.
An independent archive and additional natural shifts are necessary to test whether the identified failure generalizes.

The controlled faults preserve labels and the satellite fallback by construction.
They isolate corrupted predictor inputs; they do not simulate the physical consequences of changing wind or temperature.
They do not estimate real fault frequencies.
These probes do not demonstrate pure covariate shift with an unchanged conditional target law.
Corrupting a measured input can change that law.
Omitting VPD also does not certify the remaining sensors, energy-balance corrections, or target measurements.
The instrument-level origin of the negative vapor-pressure exports remains unresolved.

The cohort selects 85 of 152 joined stations with complete covariates.
This selection can favor particular sensors, climates, and land covers.
The archive does not establish irrigated status for every cropland station.
Available measurements at flux towers do not establish covariate availability at uninstrumented fields.
The upstream satellite products may incorporate development knowledge from this archive; withholding applies to the additional MLET models.

\section{Conclusion}
Selective neural correction requires a comparison with the fallback's error.
In this ET archive, a cross-fitted benefit selector fails under a controlled wind-input shift even after the anomalous VPD feature is removed.
Support screening reduces that failure mainly by rejecting more corrections.
At equal acceptance, support does not decisively outrank ensemble disagreement, while its advantage over the fitted benefit ranking is specific to the spatial wind probe.
These results identify a testable weakness in selective residual modeling and preserve the cases that do not confirm it.

\section{The algebra does not create a new representation method}
Suppose a linear model uses standardized input $x\in\R^p$ and isotropic ridge penalty.
For an orthogonal matrix $Q$, set $z=Q^\top x$ and $\theta=Q^\top\beta$.
Then $\theta^\top z=\beta^\top x$ and $\|\theta\|_2^2=\|\beta\|_2^2$.
The prediction and ridge objective are unchanged under this rotation.
A numerical test verifies held-out prediction equivalence within $10^{-11}$ relative and $10^{-12}$ absolute tolerance.
Thus, simply rotating linear features into orthogonal components cannot supply an improvement here.
Additional constraints, nonlinear predictors, or a changed training objective would require separate controlled experiments.
This observation prevents an unsupported attribution of novelty to factorization terminology.

For a mixture of station populations, the moments in Equation~\ref{eq:risk} also mix:
\begin{equation}
A=\sum_s w_s A_s,\qquad B=\sum_s w_s B_s.
\end{equation}
Changing $w_s$ can change both the optimal correction weight and the sign of a full correction's benefit.
Pooled scoring uses $w_s=n_s/N$; station-macro scoring uses $w_s=1/S$.
This provides a direct explanation for the weighting sensitivity without requiring a change in predictions.
It is an algebraic identity, not a new theorem about distribution shift.

\section{The retained reference-ETo outlook}\label{app:outlook}
The original MLET outlook visualizations remain part of the project and appear here without replacing their scientific meaning.
They describe reference evapotranspiration, separately from the actual-ET target of the neural correction experiment.
The American Society of Civil Engineers Environmental and Water Resources Institute (ASCE-EWRI) specifies the standardized reference calculation \citep{asce2005}.
The Global Ensemble Forecast System version 12 (GEFSv12), provided by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), supplies forecast meteorology.
The U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) AgriMet archive supplies the published station-derived target, grass-reference evapotranspiration (ETos).

\subsection{Target, issue, and code provenance}
The issue is July 3, 2019 at 00Z (00:00 UTC), where UTC means Coordinated Universal Time.
The recorded \path{source_issue_at} identifies that historical forecast issue.
The \path{archive_available_at} value is a later retrieval timestamp and does not prove operational availability at the historical issue time.
The AgriMet retrieval timestamp also does not prove its original publication time.
The independent prior-station climatology uses strictly prior calendar years for the target day of year.
The target date, not the issue date, sets the climatology year.

The implementation remains in \path{scripts/decode_gefs_reforecast.py}, \path{src/mlet/sources/gefs_reforecast_batch.py}, \path{src/mlet/sources/gefs_grib.py}, and \path{src/mlet/sources/gefs_reforecast.py}.
Reference ET and spatial aggregation remain in \path{src/mlet/outlook/eto.py} and \path{src/mlet/outlook/spatial.py}.
The frozen source receipts use SHA-256 (a 256-bit secure hash).
None of the new selective regression results alters the outlook's release gate.



The one-issue candidate passes the source count, member count, lead count,
quantile order, local-day, station-history, target-time, and grid-distance
checks. BOII (the Boise, Idaho AgriMet weather-station identifier) is a
retrospective reforecast diagnostic.

Its \path{source_issue_at} is 2019-07-03 00Z. Its \path{archive_available_at}
is \path{2026-08-04T18:08:54.243122Z}. The latter is a later archive retrieval
timestamp. It does not prove operational availability at the historical issue
time. Figure~\ref{fig:boii} shows its trajectory.
The case has one issue, one station, and $n=\FeasibilityN{}$ daily targets.

The p50 MAE is \FeasibilityMAE{} $\mmday$. The fixed station and target-day
climatology, using strictly prior calendar years, has MAE
\FeasibilityBaselineMAE{} $\mmday$. The signed
baseline-minus-forecast MAE difference is \FeasibilityImprovement{} $\mmday$,
so forecast MAE is higher. The p50 RMSE is \FeasibilityRMSE{} $\mmday$, and
the bias is \FeasibilityBias{} $\mmday$.

Empirical p10-to-p90 coverage is \FeasibilityEmpiricalCoverage{}, and the
nominal p10-to-p90 coverage target is \FeasibilityNominalCoverage{}. The mean
p10-to-p90 band width is \FeasibilityWidth{} $\mmday$. The mean
three-quantile pinball loss is \FeasibilityPinball{} $\mmday$.

These values have no sampling interval. The case has only
\FeasibilityClusters{} bootstrap cluster. It also has one target per occupied
lead cell, below the frozen minimum of \SupportMinimum. The result is a
negative feasibility diagnostic, not an estimate of general forecast skill.

Figure~\ref{fig:support} makes the incomplete state explicit. The real archive
supplies one target in each JJA, fold-2 lead cell. This is \FeasibilityN{} cell
observations. The frozen gate needs \SupportMinimumTotal{} observations when
every one of the \SupportCells{} cells reaches its minimum. The present archive
therefore supports no full-hindcast claim.

The four season groups are December-January-February (DJF), March-April-May (MAM), June-July-August (JJA), and September-October-November (SON).
The displayed band is an uncalibrated ensemble quantile band.
Validation complete means that all required support cells meet their minimum count; the present diagnostic does not meet that condition.
A skillful forecast additionally requires the frozen error-improvement criteria and an independent release review.
